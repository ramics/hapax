# Subtract the known, then characterise what is left

Berlin wastewater SRR23973193, 5 seeds x 100,000 read pairs, measured 2026-11-20.
Scripts: `experiments/subtract_known.py`, `experiments/residue_report.py`,
`experiments/subsample_uniref.py`, aggregated by `experiments/report_subtract.py`.
Prior art and what is already occupied: `results/subtract_known/PRIOR_ART.md`.

The thesis under test is Imo's, in his words on 2026-11-20: *find the absolute most
efficient way to extract the known, then score the unknown*. "Efficient" is measured in
both senses at once - bytes of index and seconds of wall time on one axis, fraction of the
library actually removed on the other.

## 1. Why subtraction and not scoring

Every single-read feature this project has tried as a novelty score has failed for one
reason: a wastewater background is coding sequence too, so "looks like a gene" cannot
separate novel-coding from known-coding. That verdict covers ORF-likeness, k-mer
composition, codon usage, GC and codon-pair bias (PR #8,
`results/readlevel_features/REPORT.md`), and translated search, where score ordering
tracks database proximity rather than novelty.

Subtraction asks the index a question an index can answer - *is this already known* - so
the polarity problem does not arise. It moves the failure somewhere else, and that is what
this experiment is built to expose: **an incomplete index makes ordinary sewage look
novel**. PR #11 measured 86% of Berlin reads with no known peptide 10-mer against a viral
plus Swiss-Prot index, and the result below says most of that was the index, not the
sewage. So no absence number here appears without the index that produced it, priced in
bytes.

## 2. The cascade

Tiers are run independently over the same reads, which supports three readings: what each
index removes alone, what they remove as a union, and what the next tier adds in a stated
order. Only the protein tier runs on the survivors of the nucleotide tiers, because
DIAMOND costs more than the rest of the cascade together; that conditioning is recorded in
the output.

Every tier's threshold is swept rather than fixed. This is not decoration. On a mock
community Kraken2's classified fraction falls from ~51% at confidence 0.05 to ~5% at the
strictest setting, so a single classified-fraction number is a threshold choice wearing a
measurement's clothes. The operating point used in the cascade is stated with every table.

## 3. Results

All percentages are of READ PAIRS, which is the occurrence-weighted unit, not an average
over distinct k-mers. The distinction matters by orders of magnitude for conserved
sequence - on curated SILVA the mean number of entries reached per distinct peptide 10-mer
is 3.49 but 388 when weighted by occurrence - and a read sees the occurrence-weighted
quantity. Nothing in this report averages over distinct k-mers.

### 3.1 The cascade, 5 seeds x 100,000 pairs

Subtrahend defined by IDENTIFICATION, not by assembly: a contig that assembles but cannot
be identified stays in play, because removing it would swallow tier 2 whole. Operating
points: SILVA and contig tiers at >=40 matching bases, Kraken2 at confidence 0.1, DIAMOND
at bitscore >=50.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| SILVA rRNA | 0.272 | 38.02 | 37.93 | 38.23 | 61.77 |

| Kraken2 Standard-8 | 8.005 | 7.52 | 0.27 | 43.71 | 56.29 |

| the sample's own rRNA contigs | 0.001 | 38.85 | 1.00 | 45.13 | 54.87 |
| the sample's own identified non-rRNA contigs | 0.015 | 67.22 | 30.33 | 75.47 | 24.53 |

Union 77.10% (77.02-77.28 across seeds). Full table with per-seed spread and every
threshold sweep: `results/subtract_recut/TABLES.md`; the earlier wholesale cut, where the
subtrahend was everything that assembled, is kept at `TABLES_wholesale.md` for comparison
(it removes 89.18% and swallows tier 2).

Three readings of that table.

**The sample's own assembly is the most efficient index by orders of magnitude.** 1.4 MB
of ribosomal contigs assembled from this very sample removes 38.85% alone, slightly more
than all 272 MB of SILVA; 15 MB of its identified non-rRNA contigs adds 30.33 points
marginally, where 16 GB of Kraken2 adds 0.42. Known-to-a-database and known-here are
different questions, and the cheap one is the second.

**Thresholds dominate database size.** PlusPF-16 removes 42.82% of the library at
confidence 0, 21.67% at 0.1 and 3.52% at 0.2 - a factor of twelve inside one database,
against a factor of 1.01 between 8 GB and 16 GB at a fixed threshold. Any published
"% unclassified" without a stated threshold is unreadable, which includes some of the
numbers this project has quoted at itself.

**The 86% unknown of PR #11 was mostly rRNA and index incompleteness.** rRNA alone is
38.02% of pairs at >=40 matching bases, and no protein index can ever match it. The
measurement agrees within about two points with the protein thread's 34.5% (36.2%
sensitivity-corrected) by exact 31-mer sharing, which is a different criterion; the
matching-base sweep gives 38.0 / 37.3 / 35.7 / 26.4 at 40 / 60 / 80 / 100 bases.

### 3.2 Ordering is a design requirement, not an accident

rRNA must be removed with a NUCLEOTIDE index before any protein index is consulted.
Against a large protein index ribosomal reads stop looking novel faster than anything
else, and the explanation that survives is annotation artefacts: public entries annotated
automatically that are really translated ribosomal sequence. The general mechanism is the
conservation multiplier measured database-independently by the now-closed protein thread;
the wastewater-specific confirmation is the wide-insert thread's entry counts, 63 of 4.1
million entries each reaching 1% or more of ribosomal reads and the top 100 carrying 52.2%
of all ribosomal read-matches, which is also a practical 63-entry blocklist. Without that
ordering a third of this library's score measures public annotation quality rather than
what is in the water. Enforced in `subtract_known.py` by tier order and asserted here
because it is a trap for anyone reimplementing the cascade.

### 3.3 Contig-level identification reverses the read-level picture

`experiments/classify_contigs.py` over this sample's 82,947 contigs (29.8 Mb), at Kraken2
confidence 0.1 or DIAMOND bitscore >=50 against Swiss-Prot and 10% of UniRef50:

- 34.78% of contigs identified, 49.13% of bases.
- Route: protein-only 26.30%, nucleotide-only 5.73%. At read length the nucleotide index
  does the work; at contig length the protein index does, by a factor of 4.6. That is the
  measured reason the cascade needs both and the reason tier 2 must be defined by
  identification.
- rRNA is only 4.38% of contigs and 3.94% of bases, at median coverage 44.7x against 1.27x
  overall. So the sample's-own-assembly step is not rRNA leakage wearing a different hat;
  the ribosomal contigs are reported as their own tier above and remove 38.85% on their
  own.

**Tier 2 is real but thin.** 46,157 contigs cannot be identified at all, 14.3 Mb, median
length 254 nt and median coverage 1.12x. Only 3.87% of them - about 1,784 contigs - clear
both 500 nt and 2x, which is where PR #5 measured that assembly and classification begin
to work at all. Tier 2 is therefore a small, honest pile rather than the easy win the
three-tier sketch implies, and its 1,784 members are the right size for the per-contig
characterisation Imo asked for.

### 3.4 Ground truth: an actual BLAST, remotely and then on our own staged copy

Two instruments, and they agree. `experiments/remote_blast_check.py` draws a stratified
sample, 60 mate-1 reads per class, labels every class locally with the cascade's own
references and thresholds, and submits them to NCBI's remote BLAST (blastn against nt, two
orders of magnitude more sequence than any index that fits here).
`experiments/report_blastcheck.py` aggregates it; a "strong hit" is 95% identity over 80%
of the query. Three clean seeds:

| class | % of library | any hit | **strong hit** | median identity | median query coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rRNA | 38.0 | 100.0 | **90.0 (86.7-93.3)** | 100.0 | 1.0 |
| public nucleotide (Kraken2) | 2.7 | 100.0 | **94.4 (93.3-95.0)** | 100.0 | 1.0 |
| identified by the sample's own assembly | 34.3 | 89.1 | **25.2 (17.8-33.3)** | 92.4 | 0.6 |
| residue, maps an unidentified contig | 8.8 | 81.1 | **9.3 (6.7-11.1)** | 89.4 | 0.4 |
| residue, maps nothing | 16.2 | 90.0 | **5.6 (3.3-6.7)** | 87.5 | 0.4 |
| tier-2 contigs (>=500 nt, >=2x) | - | 100.0 | **3.9 (3.3-5.0)** | 87.1 | 0.1 |

The headline is a correction to our own language. **The residue is not matchless.** 81-90%
of residue reads hit something, so "matches nothing" was always shorthand for "matches
nothing in an index that fits on this disk". What separates the classes is not whether they
hit but how much of the read is covered at what identity: a ribosomal read aligns over its
whole length at 100%, a residue read over a third of itself at 88%. That is the
distant-partial-homology signature, and it is why the per-read report carries closest
relative as a continuous field and never thresholds it.

**Two of five seeds are excluded, by a key rather than by judgement.** Seeds 1 and 2 were
written before failed NCBI batches were dropped from the denominator, so their real misses
and their lost queries are the same number and cannot now be separated - seed 2 reported
50% any-hit for a class that sits at 100%, which is how it was caught. Their files lack
`dropped_failed_batch`, which is the test the aggregator applies; both are re-running. Seeds
3 and 4 lost batches too and dropped them correctly, which is why two of their classes have
n=45.

#### Our own staged copy of core_nt: what the biggest nucleotide database is worth

The remote service caps the sample at 60 per class, rate-limits, and runs against a database
that moves under us with no version to cite. So a copy of NCBI's prebuilt `core_nt` (318.8
GB, pinned at prefix `2026-10-22-01-05-01`) was staged in `us-east-1` and searched directly
(`experiments/bigindex_search.py`, `experiments/bigindex_report.py`). The first pass is 500
residue mate-1 reads of block 1:

| readout | residue reads against core_nt |
|---|---|
| any hit, at BLAST's default E <= 10 (**not a significance test**; see below) | 86.8% (83.6-89.5) |
| strong alignment (>=80% identity over >=80% of the read) | 10.2% (7.8-13.2) |
| **identified at the cascade's own operating point** (>=90% identity over >=40 bases) | **7.8% (5.8-10.5)** |
| median identity of the best hit | 88.6% |
| median fraction of the read aligned | 0.374 |

Best-hit identity piles up at 80-90% (211 of 434 hits) while the aligned fraction piles up
between a quarter and a half of the read (298 of 434), which this report first read as "a
typical residue read's closest relative in all of core_nt is a ~40-base partial match at
~88% identity".

**That reading is withdrawn. It describes chance matches.** The rows carry an E-value and
this table did not read it; BLAST's reporting default is 10, so every figure above includes
alignments that are not significant at all. Of the 334 partial-but-unidentified reads the
median E-value is **0.24**, 45.5% sit between 0.05 and 1, and 24.6% are at E >= 1 - which is
what a 107 nt nucleotide query finds by chance in 318.8 GB. Demanding significance inverts
the shape:

| readout, at E < 1e-5 | residue reads against core_nt |
|---|---|
| reads with a significant hit | **73 of 500 (14.6%)** |
| median fraction of the read aligned, among those | **0.96** |
| median identity, among those | 90.1% |
| significant *and* partial (aligned fraction < 0.95) | 28 of 500 (5.6%), at 83% identity, aligning a median of **87 of 107 bases** |
| the 39 identified at the operating point | 105 of 107 bases at 97.7%, median E 6.6e-40 |

**So a real relative aligns end to end, and partial alignment is the signature of a chance
match rather than of divergence.** Note what the 28 survivors are not: they align 87 of 107
bases, so even the significant partial matches are not the "half matches, half is gibberish"
shape requirement 1.ii was written about. At read level that shape has no population left. The operating point itself is unharmed - everything it
identifies is significant by four orders of magnitude - and requirement 1.iii is answered
more cleanly than before: 14.6% of residue reads have a relative in core_nt, typically ~90%
identical over their whole length, which is a divergent strain rather than novel material;
~85% have no significant relative in the largest nucleotide database there is.

The mechanism is the one Imo named on 2026-11-21: a 107 nt nucleotide query has almost no
statistical power against a database this size unless it matches end to end. It is also why
the small significant-and-partial pile matters out of proportion to its size - it is the only
population where the reference-boundary explanation (a spliced transcript against a genomic
subject, a truncated record) can be tested at all, and
`experiments/partial_alignment_eyeball.py` renders it for inspection in
`results/partial_eyeball/`.

**Rule this cost us: never quote a BLAST identity without its E-value.** Same family as the
fixed cost quoted as a rate (section 3.6) - an instrument reporting a default that the
analysis forgot to constrain.

**The efficiency comparison is the result.** 318.8 GB of the world's nucleotide sequence
removes about 8 points of the residue at the operating point the rest of the curve uses. 15
MB of this sample's own identified contigs removes 30 points (section 3.1). Four orders of
magnitude of index for a quarter of the effect, now measured against the real database
rather than inferred from what fits on this disk.

Limits: one block, mate 1 only, 500 reads, so this is a pilot and not the table. Five
6,000-read blocks and a cheap-task comparison are running; `results/bigindex/` carries the
rows, the timing and the operating point for each.

### 3.5 Does the removal curve flatten? Not on this evidence

The cascade's three Kraken2 sizes (0.67, 8, 16 GB) gain 0.42 points on the last doubling,
which reads like exhaustion. The protein thread made exactly that mistake in the other
direction - two small virus-weighted points read as "protein databases barely help",
overturned when a tenth of UniRef50 moved the same quantity 23 points - so three points on
a line are not a curve here either.

`experiments/nucleotide_ladder.py` puts more points on the nucleotide axis without
building databases this environment cannot build: classify once against the largest
database available, then ask what removal would have been had only a nested random subset
of REFERENCE TAXA been present. The axis is reference content, not bytes. Both biases run
the same way - deleting calls loses the reads a smaller real database would still place on
a retained relative, and Kraken2's capped hash means bytes grow sublinearly with taxa - so
each point understates, which makes this a conservative test.

| reference content | taxa | removed, conf 0.0 | marginal | removed, conf 0.1 | marginal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3% | 6 | 0.05 | 0.05 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 1% | 20 | 0.24 | 0.20 | 0.05 | 0.04 |
| 3% | 57 | 1.00 | 0.75 | 0.15 | 0.11 |
| 10% | 202 | 3.68 | 2.68 | 1.49 | 1.34 |
| 30% | 642 | 9.42 | 5.75 | 2.60 | 1.11 |
| 50% | 1,086 | 16.88 | 7.46 | 6.37 | 3.77 |
| 100% | 2,155 | 42.82 | 25.94 | 21.67 | 15.30 |

No plateau appears: the last doubling of content still buys 15-26 points. **But the
instrument is weak and the reason is worth stating.** Reads are heavily concentrated over
reference taxa - at confidence 0.1 the single top taxon carries 49.0% of classified reads,
the top ten 86.4% - so most of that last step is one dominant taxon being inside the
subset rather than outside it. This ladder supports "has not visibly saturated" and does
not resolve the curve's shape; the per-seed spread (min-max in
`results/nucleotide_ladder/TABLES.md`) is wide for the same reason and is reported rather
than averaged away.

The stronger nucleotide evidence is section 3.4's BLAST: against a database two orders of
magnitude larger than anything local, 87% of residue reads find a partial, distant match.
Sequence our indexes miss is findable in a database 70 times larger, which is what "the
curve has not flattened" means operationally. On the protein axis the same question is
measured directly on this cascade's residue in `results/residue_protladder/`.

### 3.6 The positive control: how divergent must a virus be to survive the cascade?

`experiments/spike_holdout.py`. Three real genomes - pepper mild mottle virus
(NC_003630.1), human astrovirus (NC_001943.1) and human rhinovirus 1 (NC_038311.1) - are
simulated into this library's own measured insert distribution (median 194, p95 363) and
its own measured error rates (0.00089 on R1, 0.00117 on R2), then walked away from
themselves by uniform substitution and pushed through every index the cascade subtracts.
2,000 pairs per genome per rung, 5 seeds.

A spike removed at tier 1 by a public database is a false negative of the whole
architecture, so this is the number that matters most.

| divergence | spike pairs surviving tier 1 |
|---|---|
| 0% | 0.0% (0.0-0.0 across seeds) |
| 5% | 0.6% (0.0-2.0) |
| 10% | 12.7% (6.4-19.2) |
| 20% | 82.2% (73.8-89.8) |
| 30% | 99.5% (98.2-100.0) |

The 0% rung is the control that the cascade works at all: a deposited virus spiked into
the library is removed completely. Recognition then falls away between 10% and 20%
nucleotide divergence, which is this pipeline's measured boundary between *already known*
and *new*.

**Which tier does the recognising** (mean % of spike pairs removed by each index alone):

| divergence | sample's contigs | Kraken2 viral | Kraken2 PlusPF-16 | viral proteins | Swiss-Prot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 65.5 | 100.0 | 64.0 | 97.5 | 97.5 |
| 5% | 51.6 | 86.6 | 5.9 | 94.6 | 94.7 |
| 10% | 21.8 | 28.1 | 0.4 | 82.9 | 83.0 |
| 20% | 0.2 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 17.4 | 17.6 |
| 30% | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.5 | 0.5 |

**The nucleotide side dies first and the protein index is the last thing standing.** By
10% divergence nucleotide identification has fallen to a quarter while translated search
still removes 84%; by 20% nucleotide is at zero and only the protein index holds anything
at all. That is the same asymmetry section 3.3 found at contig level, now measured as a
function of distance rather than of length, and it is the argument for keeping a protein
tier in a cascade whose cheapest and most effective tiers are nucleotide.

**Two of the three genomes turned out to be in this sample already.** Pepper mild mottle
virus assembles here at 285x coverage and human astrovirus as a 6,823 nt contig at 11.7x;
human rhinovirus 1 does not appear in this sample's assembly at all. That was discovered
while checking an abundance reading that looked like a sketch artefact and was not: a
PMMoV read recurs in the library because PMMoV is genuinely abundant in sewage. It is
reported here rather than quietly fixed because it changes how the 0% rung reads - part of
that removal is the sample recognising itself - and because rhinovirus is therefore the
clean holdout of the three. The survival ladder is unaffected in shape: the per-tier table
above separates the sample's own contigs from the public indexes, and the two columns fall
away together.

**Limits of this control.** Uniform substitution is a crude model of real divergence -
selection shapes it, codon structure constrains it, synonymous sites carry most of it - so
the percentages are ordinal, not family distances. No indels are applied, deliberately: a
frameshift would collapse the coding fields for reasons unrelated to how far away a
relative is.

**At 1 in a million.** A virus at that abundance contributes about 8.7 read pairs in this
8,731,224-pair run. Per-read survival above says roughly one of them reaches the per-read
report at 10% divergence (8.7 x 0.127 = 1.1 pairs) and seven at 20% (8.7 x 0.822 = 7.2). Per-read behaviour is measured at a spike rate
high enough to resolve it and the 1-in-a-million statement is arithmetic on top; nothing
here was measured at that rate directly, because a sample that holds 8.7 pairs cannot
resolve a rate. That was the error of PR #11, where 1-per-million thresholds came from
zero counts in a 20,000-read run, and it is not repeated here. The one field whose value
really does depend on abundance - library recurrence - is measured with exactly the number
of spike pairs 1-in-a-million implies:

| | median 31-mer count in 400,000 pairs |
|---|---|
| pepper mild mottle virus (abundant here) | 10, 18, 23, 18, 7 across seeds |
| human astrovirus | 1 in every seed |
| human rhinovirus 1 (the clean holdout) | 1 in every seed |
| a typical background read | 67.6 |

So a virus at 1 in a million reads as a singleton against a background whose typical read
recurs about 68 times, and the field does separate the two. PMMoV does not, because it is
genuinely abundant in sewage - which is the field working, not failing. The count-min
sketch only overstates (saturation 0.006%), so a read it calls rare is rare for certain.
The sketch block is the same 400,000 pairs in every seed by design, since it stands for
"the library", so the seeds vary the spike and not the background: the background number
is one measurement, not five.

### 3.7 "Half a read matches high, the other half is gibberish" - what is actually going on

Requirement 1.ii, measured by `experiments/partial_alignment_anatomy.py`, which takes
partially-matching sequence and asks which of four things it is, with the reads the cascade
*identified* as a control population throughout. The four:

- **A, chimaera**: the unmatched piece aligns to a *different* molecule assembled in this
  sample. That is the hypothesis Imo named, and it is the only one of the four that is a
  statement about the library rather than about the database.
- **B, divergent continuation**: the unmatched piece returns to the *same* subject, more
  weakly. One molecule, half of it past the search's recognition floor.
- **C, artefact end**: the unmatched piece is low-entropy, homopolymeric or adapter.
- **D, short true homology**: nothing fires - the match is simply short.

**At read level this question is unanswerable, and that is the result.** At 107 nt, when a
read matches over ~74% of itself the unmatched remainder is about 10 aa, below any protein
search's floor, so the discriminating test returns nothing for 99.6% of reads. The split-
mapping test fires on 13.4% of partial residue reads but 37.4% of already-identified ones:
it measures mappability, not chimaerism. Partial alignment is not even enriched in the
residue (23.8% vs 19.7%) - it is a property of short reads meeting a protein database. What
separates the two piles is identity, continuously: 51.9% vs 63.2%.

**At contig level it is answerable, and the answer is a negative on chimaerism.** Five
seeds, each 800 unidentifiable and 800 identified contigs of >=500 nt sampled at random,
DIAMOND blastp against Swiss-Prot, split segments re-queried. Spread across seeds in
brackets:

| | unidentifiable | identified (control) |
|---|---|---|
| any protein hit | 595/800 | 797/800 |
| mean aligned fraction of the contig | 0.315 (0.304-0.327) | 0.567 (0.546-0.593) |
| mean identity | 35.0% (34.5-35.4) | 40.6% (39.5-41.5) |
| partial hits (15-75% aligned) | 85% of hits | 69% |
| A chimaera | **0.04% (0.00-0.20)** | **0.15% (0.00-0.19)** |
| B divergent continuation | 0.39% (0.20-0.59) | 3.12% (2.45-4.09) |
| C artefact end | 0.59% (0.39-0.79) | 1.27% (0.75-1.78) |
| D short true homology | 98.98% | 95.50% |

So: **no evidence that the unmatched part of a partially-matching contig comes from another
molecule present in this sample.** The chimaera category fires on 0.04% of unidentifiable
contigs and 0.15% of identified ones - indistinguishable at these counts, and if anything
higher in the control, where a mis-assembly would be just as visible. The limit is
explicit: this test can only see a chimaera whose partner also assembled here, so it cannot
exclude a join to something too rare to assemble.

B is the instructive one. Divergent continuation fires eight times *more often* on
already-identified contigs than on unidentifiable ones (3.12% against 0.39%, no overlap
between the seed ranges), so it flags familiarity, not novelty: you only observe a weak
return to the same subject when the subject is well represented to begin with. A pipeline
that read B as "divergent, therefore interesting" would be reading database coverage
backwards. The same inversion appears in the coverage-shape fields of section 5.2, measured
by a different instrument on different data.

What does separate the piles is simply how much matches at all - a continuous quantity,
0.327 against 0.593 aligned fraction - and not any categorical verdict about the unmatched
remainder. This is also the first field measured here that works on contigs and fails on
reads, which is an argument about where in a production tool this work belongs.

Five seeds, 8,000 contigs in total. The chimaera rate is quoted as "indistinguishable
between the piles at a few parts in a thousand" rather than as zero, because one seed in
five found two instances in each pile: a rate that low cannot be separated from the
segment-matching threshold's own false-positive rate.

### 3.8 What a bigger index would cost to search, and the one number we cannot measure here

`experiments/diamond_scaling.py`, because a cost estimate for a bigger index has to rest on
a throughput measurement and the obvious one is the wrong one. The protein ladder's runs put
8,000 pairs against four databases in about half an hour, and multiplying that up to a
sample's ~2 million residue pairs would have overstated the cost enormously: at 8,000
queries a DIAMOND run is almost entirely the one-off database scan.

Measured on two database sizes and two modes, three query counts each, four threads:

| database | mode | fixed scan | marginal per read | 2,000 reads | 32,000 reads |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.36 GB | default | 12.3 s | 0.00002 s | 12.3 s | 13.0 s |
| 0.36 GB | --sensitive | 66.1 s | 0.00022 s | 66.5 s | 73.1 s |
| 1.82 GB | default | 8.7 s | 0.00026 s | 10.1 s | 17.3 s |
| 1.82 GB | --sensitive | 64.3 s | 0.00309 s | 68.7 s | 162.7 s |

The fits are clean - residuals of a second or two on runs of one to three minutes - and two
things follow immediately. **The ladder's runs were database-bound**: 16x the queries cost
6% more time on the small database, so per-read cost at ladder scale says nothing about
per-read cost at production scale. And **`--sensitive` costs 10 to 14x default**, which is
a real design choice rather than a free switch.

**The scaling in database size is superlinear and that is where the honesty has to go.** A
5x larger database multiplies the marginal cost per read by 14.2 in sensitive mode and 13.2
in default - an exponent of about 1.65, and the two modes agreeing on it is more than a
coincidence would give. But it is still two points. Taking UniRef100 at ~100 GB, 55x the
larger database measured here, the two candidate laws disagree by a factor of 15:

- linear in bytes: 0.17 s per read sensitive, so about 94 hours for 2 M pairs on four
  threads; default mode about 8 hours.
- exponent 1.65: 2.5 s per read sensitive, which is 1,400 hours and not a plan; default
  mode about 117 hours.

So the far-end search cost is NOT quoted here, and the ladder's lesson is the reason: this
project has already read two points as a curve once. What resolves it is a third point on
real hardware, which is why the big-index run's first act is a timed pilot against the real
UniRef50 at 18 GB - a database five times larger than anything measurable in this
container, and the point that separates the two laws. The pilot costs minutes and the guide
requires a measured baseline check anyway.

One design consequence either way. The deliverable here is identity to the closest
relative, including distant relatives, which is exactly what sensitivity buys - so
`--sensitive` cannot simply be dropped to make the cost fit. The split that follows from
these numbers is default mode for the subtraction pass over every read, and sensitive mode
on the sampled population where the identity DISTRIBUTION is the product.

## 4. What the residue is, and is not

The 22.90% residue breaks down as follows, measured by mapping it back to assemblies
rather than to public databases:

- **8.42% abundant-but-unplaceable** (8.33-8.51 across seeds): assembles here, cannot be
  identified against anything. This is tier 2's read-level population and the pile where
  longer contigs and better classifiers would help.
- **9.97% maps a contig from one of the other 115 samples** of the same study
  (9.86-10.04). Observed, deliberately NOT subtracted: subtracting it would make
  cross-sample recurrence zero by construction and destroy a field the per-read report
  needs. The two categories above overlap, so they do not sum.
- **10.90% genuinely missing** (10.76-11.07): nothing in public databases, nothing in this
  sample's own assembly, nothing in the other 115 samples' assemblies. Rare AND
  unsequenced, which is also the description of a novel virus at 1 in a million - so our
  target sits on the same side of this feature as the irreducible background, and that is
  a limit of the architecture rather than of the implementation.

Subtracting the sample's OWN data is what turns the residue from "merely undeposited" into
"genuinely rare". That is the narrower replacement for the claim withdrawn in PR #13
("absence in wastewater means wastewater"), which failed because the organisms were in the
sample's own contigs, just not in public databases.

The residue is also, by construction, enriched for artefacts: a pile defined by matching
nothing collects adapters, primer dimers, chimaeras and low-complexity sequence. The
cheap artefact tier removes 0.31% of the library before any database is consulted, and
every residue row carries its own artefact fields plus the mean error probability from its
quality string, so an unexplainable read can be read as "probably instrument" rather than
"probably biology".

## 5. The per-read evidence report

The third tier. Every read the cascade could not identify gets a row of evidence rather
than a verdict: closest relative as a number per index, whether it looks coding, GC,
repetitiveness, dinucleotide bias, folding energy against a dinucleotide-preserving
shuffle, library abundance, recurrence across the other samples, and the mate's agreement.
"Cannot discriminate here" is a legitimate value in every column, and a shuffled null sits
beside each field so a reader can see what the field does on sequence that cannot be
interesting. `experiments/field_independence.py` reports how many independent measurements
the table really contains, and how much each field separates the residue from the reads the
cascade identified, because independence and usefulness are different properties.

### 5.1 The two families pull opposite ways, and neither is both strong and correctly signed

Stated here as a structural property of the report rather than being rediscovered field by
field, which is how it was found. Jointly with the thread measuring whether the residue is
structured:

- The **absence family** - peptide absence, `zero_frac`, "no hit in any index" - has the
  right polarity. Absence is what a genuinely new molecule produces. It is also weak:
  peptide absence dies at 1 per million (160,774 false flags per million at the strictest
  expressible threshold, PR #11 and #13), and `zero_frac` is the weakest of the five
  coverage axes at 0.617 AUC.
- The **coverage-shape family** - within-read coverage spread, the largest within-read
  step, mate disagreement - is strong, 0.76 to 0.78 AUC each, and has the WRONG polarity.
  Measured in `results/cov_step_conservation/`: the step tracks how many distinct contigs
  of the sample's own assembly a read shares sequence with (+0.577, surviving inside
  `log_cov` quartiles at +0.447, +0.389, +0.392, +0.242), and high-step reads align to 2.23
  distinct contigs against 0.65. It is measuring witnesses, so it fires on sequence shared
  between molecules. That is a familiarity statement.
- The same inversion appears independently in the partial-match anatomy of section 3.7,
  where divergent continuation fires six times more often on already-identified contigs
  than on unidentifiable ones.

### 5.2 Which coverage axes enter the report, and in what role

Settled jointly with the thread that measured them, on evidence from both sides rather than
by preference. `experiments/shape_axis_ladder.py` ran the mechanism test over all 99,745
scorable pairs of block 1, with a `log_cov` band fixed once at rung zero and reused at
every rung, because a falling AUC would otherwise be indistinguishable from a bigger index
simply removing abundant reads:

| cumulative subtrahend | `cov_step` overall | `cov_step` inside the fixed band |
|---|---|---|
| SILVA, 1.12 GB | 0.248 | 0.317 (0.309-0.324) |
| + the sample's own rRNA contigs | 0.244 | 0.320 (0.312-0.327) |
| + its own identified contigs | 0.213 | **0.494 (0.480-0.509)** |
| + all 116 samples' 1 kb contigs | 0.117 | 0.467 (0.431-0.504) |

AUC is oriented so that "not identified scores higher", so below 0.5 is the familiarity
polarity. Overall separation strengthens as the subtrahend grows while separation inside
the band goes to chance, and `log_cov`'s own overall AUC strengthens over the same rungs
(0.141 to 0.061). That is the signature of a confound plus a real effect: the overall gain
is abundance, and the shape axes' own power leaves with the witnessed material, which is
what "they are measuring witnesses" predicts. The in-band unidentified arm falls to 1,592
pairs at the own-contigs rung and 237 at the last, so the claim rests on the own-contigs
rung and the last rung is quoted as a direction; Hanley-McNeil intervals are in the JSON
for every cell.

`mate_disagree` behaved differently in that test (-0.200 overall but +0.013 inside the
band), and the sibling thread's follow-up explains it: 38.2% of pairs with
`mate_disagree >= 2` have exactly one mate entirely unwitnessed against 7.0% of residue
pairs generally, which is the error-versus-absence distinction this project already relies
on - instrument error is independent between mates, organism-level absence is shared. That
is why subtraction does not remove it.

So the three roles are:

- **`mate_disagree`: the per-sample error-floor estimator**, SPEC section 4.2's fourth
  axis. Novelty-neutral, and it earns its place by measuring the floor rather than the
  signal. Its calibration has been run and it PASSES, which no other field here can say:
  the clean single-substitution signature - a 25-40 k-mer zero-run in exactly one mate
  while the other has none - reads 2.33% of the reads the cascade identified and 2.46% of
  the residue, the same number in both, which is what a database-independent error floor
  must look like. The absence half of the same measurement behaves oppositely, 2.99%
  against 6.97%, so one population has more missing molecules and neither has more errors:
  the error-versus-organism split measured rather than argued from mate independence. The
  caveat ships with it - this library's rates imply 9.1% of R1 and 11.8% of R2 reads carry
  at least one error, so the clean signature catches about a quarter of them, and the
  equality between populations is the readout rather than the level. It is also the only
  one of the five axes whose mechanism does not depend on what is in a database, which
  makes it the only one that can be expected to mean the same thing in a second city.
- **`cov_step` and `cov_spread`: description, labelled familiarity.** They are strong and
  they point the wrong way, so they describe a read and must never be read as evidence
  that it is new.
- **`log_cov`: not in any score.** It is partly definitional with respect to what an index
  removes, and it dominates every pooled number it is allowed into.

### 5.3 Does this library get grittier towards one end? Measured twice, and the ramp is small

Illumina reads usually degrade towards the 3' end, and a reader will assume it. This library
was measured once as flat, which is a thin basis for a fact every quality-trusting field
rests on, so `experiments/quality_profile.py` re-measures it across five flow-cell blocks
and measures it a second way that does not trust the quality strings at all - necessary
because NovaSeq bins quality, so a flat Q profile is not by itself evidence of a flat error
rate.

**The quality profile: a decline, but a small one.** Mean Q runs 36.4 to 36.7 along mate 1
with a last-quarter-minus-first-quarter change of -0.14 Q (-0.22 to -0.09 across seeds),
and 36.7 down to 36.3 along mate 2, change -0.31 Q (-0.44 to -0.23). So the ramp is real,
reproducible, and worth about 0.3 of a Q unit out of 36.5. It shows up more clearly in the
tail of the distribution than in the mean: the fraction of bases below Q20 rises from 0.5%
to about 1.3% along mate 2 (+0.0063) and by +0.0026 along mate 1.

**The reference-free instrument agrees.** 26-27% of pairs overlap their own mate, so every
base in the overlap is observed twice and a mismatch means one observation is wrong. The
combined mismatch rate is 0.000796 (0.000678-0.000885 over five seeds), flat along the body
of the read at 0.00068, rising to 0.0019 at the final base - a factor of about three, on an
absolute scale of a tenth of a percent.

Two things that measurement also says. The apparent 2% mismatch rate at mate 1's first six
positions is an artefact of which molecules can reach them: a pair only overlaps at position
0 when its insert is under about 93 nt, so those positions rest on 464-646 observations from
the shortest inserts alone, against 3,000-5,000 elsewhere. And the Q strings imply a
combined error rate of 0.00206 where the overlap instrument measures 0.000796, so the quality
scores here are conservative by about 2.6x - or the overlapping short-insert subset is
cleaner than the library, and this test cannot separate those two. Either way the project's
quoted rates of 0.00089 (R1) and 0.00117 (R2) are Q-derived and should be read as an upper
bound.

**The asymmetry that matters is between mates, and most of it was length.** Among pairs
where exactly one mate maps to the sample's own assembly, 62.1% have mate 2 as the unmapped
one - but mate 2 is 93 nt against mate 1's 107 after the non-template prefix trim, and a
shorter read maps less often for reasons unrelated to quality. Truncating mate 1 to the same
length puts it at 53.9% (52.3-55.4 across seeds). So the real mate asymmetry is about 1.17x,
not the 1.5x the raw number suggests.

That has a direct consequence for section 5.2's error-floor field, which counts pairs where
exactly one mate is unwitnessed and treats the two mates as interchangeable: it will fire
about 1.17x more often on mate 2 than on mate 1. The field is still usable - the calibration
that matters is the equality of the floor between the residue and the identified reads, and
that comparison is unaffected because both populations carry the same mate asymmetry - but
the floor should be reported per mate, and a 54:46 split between mates must not be read as
biology.

The general rule this is an instance of lives in `CLAUDE.md` under Conventions - the mates
of this library are not interchangeable, because of the 14 nt non-template trim - so that
whoever builds the next pair-symmetric field meets it before they build it rather than after.
What follows here is the worked example.

The pooled 1.17x turned out to hide something, which the sibling thread measured on the
k-mer side: **the two halves of that field lean towards opposite mates, and both leans are
length artefacts.** Asymmetric absence falls on mate 2 (64:36 in identified pairs, 60:40 in
the residue) because mate 2 has 62 scorable 31-mers against mate 1's 76 after the trim, so
the shorter mate comes up empty more easily. The single-error signature falls on mate 1
(58:42 and 55:45) because a 25-40 k-mer zero-run needs a long enough profile to sit in at
all. A single pooled ratio would have averaged those into something meaningless. The
equality between populations survives both, since both populations carry both asymmetries.

**What the position-independence result licenses.** The flat body matters for more than
describing the library: it is what makes a 25-40 k-mer zero-run a clean single-substitution
signature. If errors clustered at the 3' end, a zero-run of that length would sit against
the read end and be truncated, and the field would be counting read ends rather than errors.
Flat at 0.00068 along the body with only the final base elevated is the condition that field
needs, and it is now measured rather than assumed.

So the report currently has no field that is both strong and pointing at novelty, and the
strong fields describe a read without saying it is new. Two consequences are carried
through the rest of this work. Every field in the table is labelled with its polarity, so a
reader cannot mistake a strong familiarity field for evidence of novelty. And the
load-bearing quantity is the one that does carry novelty polarity continuously - identity
to the closest relative, which separates every population measured here (section 3.4) and
which section 3.7 found to be the only separator that survives at contig level.

## 6. Calibration and limits

Stated up front because every number here is one-sided.

- **Removal is a lower bound on "known".** An index cannot distinguish sequence with no
  relative anywhere from sequence whose relative is below the matching threshold. The
  sweeps are the honest form of that limit.
- **Subtraction is not free.** The SURPI+ evaluation measured pre-processing removing
  0.43% of true viral reads. Any read this cascade removes could in principle be a
  divergent member of a known family, and at the 1-in-a-million target even a small
  false-removal rate matters more than the headline removal does.
- **The far end of the index axis is extrapolated, not measured.** A DIAMOND database of
  full UniRef50 is ~30 GB against ~15 GB of writable disk left after the nucleotide
  indexes, so UniRef50 enters as streamed nested 2% and 10% subsets. The shape of removal
  against index size is measured; the value at 100% is an extrapolation and is labelled as
  one. A run against the far end needs disk this
  container does not have; the streaming recipe in `experiments/subsample_uniref.py`
  already does the hard part. Hardware for it has been offered (us-east-1, spot, up to
  2 TB of EBS, 8 vCPU and about 64 GB of RAM), so the far end is now a decision rather
  than a wall - see the next point.
- **Which database the far end should be, and why it is not nr.** Scaling from the built
  10% subset (1.82 GB of DIAMOND database for 4.12 M records), UniRef50 is ~18 GB,
  UniRef90 ~56 GB and UniRef100 ~100 GB; a DIAMOND nr database is a few hundred. With the
  disk ceiling at 2 TB all four fit, so nr is affordable and the question is whether it is
  worth it. **The plan is UniRef100, not nr, and this is a claim about content rather than
  about cost:** TrEMBL is translated GenBank/EMBL/DDBJ CDS, so nr's protein content is
  largely UniProt's, clustered differently; what nr adds over UniRef100 is redundancy,
  PDB and RefSeq model proteins. That is an argument, not a measurement, so it gets a
  measurement: the remote NCBI translated pass runs the same sampled reads against the
  real nr, and if its identity distribution matches UniRef100's on those reads, nr buys
  nothing here and the cheaper index is the right one. If the distributions differ, nr
  goes on the disk that is now available. Either way the report will say which, and the
  binding constraint is no longer disk but RAM at ~64 GB and search time, which section
  3.8's scaling measurement prices.
- **The curve has not flattened.** The protein side's absence-versus-index-size curve was
  still buying about 20 points per tenfold step at the largest index reachable (PR #10
  sections 4.6-4.7), so "removal per gigabyte and where it flattens" currently has no
  flattening point and this report does not invent one. The nucleotide analogue is
  measured in section 3.5 rather than assumed from three Kraken2 points.
- **The all-sample assembly tier is a lower bound**: the GEO file truncates mid-stream at
  sample S65 of 116 (recorded in `data/MANIFEST.md`), so that tier subtracts less than a
  complete file would.
- **Read-level abundance is relative to a sample of the run**, 400,000 pairs, not a genome
  coverage depth. A count-min sketch only ever overstates a count, so a read called rare
  is rare for certain - the error runs in the safe direction for that claim.
